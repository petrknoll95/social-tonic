# What beverage names can do—and what they cannot

**Created 2026-10-17T11:07:23+02:00 · Europe/Prague (UTC+02:00)**
**Research cut-off: 17 October 2026 · Desk research; no final name, consumer test or trademark clearance**

## Findings for this project

A useful name helps people identify, remember, say and find a brand, and can suggest a relevant experience. It works together with the product, descriptor, packaging and repeated exposure. The research does **not** establish a naming style that reliably produces sales, a universal ideal length, or a set of sounds that makes a Czech/EU drink successful.

The strongest practical direction is to evaluate **a distinctive verbal identity beside a clear, localised beverage descriptor**, then test both separately and together. Keep the name's promise compatible with the actual formula. Occasion, social identity, metaphor and approachable coinage are viable territories to explore, alongside narrower descriptive or ingredient-led challengers once positioning is settled. No territory wins by default.

For this Czech-founded project, prioritise spoken recovery and natural conversation in Czech, then test the same jobs in genuinely intended expansion markets. A short English word may be harder to spell from hearing than a longer Czech word. An emotional association can disappear in translation. Names implying calm, cognition, naturalness or zero alcohol require review of their full presentation; moving the promise into a product family does not remove that obligation.

The comparative sample contains **48 names: 12 consumer brands outside beverages and 36 beverage brands**, including established, emerging, unknown-scale and discontinued examples. It is deliberately varied, not representative. Commercial outcomes are separately documented in [commercial-evidence.csv](data/commercial-evidence.csv); all name coding appears in [brand-name-analysis.csv](data/brand-name-analysis.csv). The six territories and handoff are in the [naming brief](supporting/naming-brief.md), with a usable [evaluation and test protocol](supporting/evaluation-and-testing.md).

## 1. Scope and evidence rules

The proposed product is a finished nonalcoholic liquid in cans or bottles, primarily CBD-free. Socialising, unwinding and a defined functional proposition remain alternatives. Formula, audience, positioning and architecture are undecided. This research therefore studies names without treating a particular ingredient, “buzz”, work-break recommendation or sleep promise as the chosen identity.

The repository's [customer research](../../../research/customers/05-segments-and-occasions/report.md), [US landscape](../../../research/market/01-us-product-landscape/report.md), [EU/Czech competitors](../../../research/market/04-eu-czech-competition/report.md), [commercial traction](../../../research/market/02-commercial-traction/report.md), [claims](../../../research/regulatory/09-claims-and-messaging/report.md), [range/format](../../../research/product/12-range-and-format/report.md), [brand/acquisition](../../../research/commercial/22-brand-and-acquisition/report.md) and [new occasions](../../../ideas/2026-10-17_10-38-12_new-drink-opportunities/report.md) supplied context and discovery leads. Their recommendations remain hypotheses. In particular, interest in an afternoon break does not establish a productivity brand, and enjoying an evening ritual does not establish a demand for a psychoactive effect. Same-repository reports are not independent replications of one another.

The naming sample is broader than a formula benchmark. Bottled aperitif/mixing products and Oatly's carton/food portfolio are included to study brand architecture; they are explicitly adjacent cases, not evidence that a can/bottle RTD has the same formulation, use occasion or economics. UK examples are labelled separately from EU examples. Geography refers to commercial context, not an assertion of availability in every country.

We use four evidence categories:

| Category | What it supports | What it does not support |
|---|---|---|
| Peer-reviewed findings | Specific experimental or observational naming mechanisms, with population/task boundaries. | Universal naming rules or forecast sales for this drink. |
| Documented company cases | A name, stated origin, product structure or dated commercial event. | A counterfactual showing the business would have failed with another name. |
| Practitioner frameworks | Useful processes, diagnostic questions and implementation practices. | Independent proof that a proprietary method creates successful businesses. |
| Analyst interpretation / hypothesis | Coding of tone, possible associations, expansion trade-offs and recommendations to test. | Founder intention, native-speaker validation or measured consumer perception. |

Unknown remains unknown. Company-reported metrics retain that provenance even when repeated by a newspaper. The [source register](sources.csv) records publication date, access date, source type, access depth, supported claims and limitations. Some papers were accessible only as abstracts and some official pages through indexed excerpts; these limits are retained rather than concealed.

## 2. Define commercial evidence before interpreting names

The selection framework was written before the pattern counts. Inclusion required a verifiable consumer-facing name and product/category evidence, plus either a scoped commercial indicator or an explicit unknown-scale classification. We selected category, geography, name-mechanism and outcome variety, including the user's discovery leads; we did not select only brief, playful or successful names.

**Established** means documented substantial commercial scale or durable multi-year brand presence, not assured profitability. **Emerging traction** means specific documented commercial expansion, sales or growth with less established scale/history. **Active, scale unknown** means an observed offer or retailer presence without adequate comparable commercial data. **Discontinued/renamed** describes a dated brand/product event. A company can have large sales and losses; a successful domestic brand can withdraw abroad. These labels are navigation aids: the underlying event rows, period and geography take precedence.

Indicators must remain separate:

- **Manufacturer revenue** differs from consumer retail sales, which include channel mark-ups and may cover selected outlets only.
- **Units** need a period, geography and unit definition; cans, cases and servings are not interchangeable.
- **Growth** needs a starting base and comparable scope; acquisition-driven portfolio growth is not organic brand growth.
- **Distribution** distinguishes announced listings, stocked doors, weighted distribution and actual sell-through.
- **Longevity/continued availability** supports persistence, not market leadership, profit or modern repeat rates.
- **Retention** requires identifiable customer cohorts, opportunity to reorder and paid repeat definitions. A subscription button is not evidence.
- **Awareness** requires a survey population and measure; followers, likes and press coverage do not replace it.
- **Acquisition** is an ownership/strategic outcome. Price includes expectations and assets; it does not reveal why consumers bought or stayed.

Funding and valuation are excluded as success criteria. They may fund distribution and advertising, which themselves confound a naming-success story.

The Liquid Death Harris Poll case study reports awareness/trial changes between Q1 and Q2 2024 among younger US adults, but is a provider case study with limited methods and an apparent sample-size typo. It does not identify the incremental impact of the name or establish measured conversion. No comparable cohort-repeat series was established across this sample. [LEAD026][LEAD026]

**Survivorship bias remains material.** Visible brands are easier to research, and surviving brands have accumulated familiarity. Two discontinued beverage names and additional historical adverse outcomes do not create a balanced control group. We cannot estimate whether a pattern is more common among winners than among all entrants, or whether it raises survival probability. A defensible answer to “is this distinctive to successful brands?” is **not established by these data**. The counterexamples refute deterministic rules, not the possibility of a modest context-dependent naming contribution.

## 3. What naming research actually supports

### Mechanisms and limits

| Mechanism and study | What was observed | Context, access and practical limit |
|---|---|---|
| Familiarity, association competition — Meyers-Levy (1989) [EMP001][EMP001] | Two studies found that word frequency moderates how association-set size relates to memory. | Abstract only; brand-information memory, not sales. Familiar words can supply useful cues and competing meanings. |
| Linguistic features and memory — Lowrey, Shrum & Dubitsky (2003) [EMP002][EMP002] | A 480-brand copytesting analysis associated semantic fit, wordplay, initial plosives and unusual spelling with aspects of memory, conditional on familiarity. | US women 18–65, national brands and TV exposure; observational combined recall/recognition outcome. It did not test spelling after audio or isolate causality. |
| Sound and perceived attributes — Yorkston & Menon (2004) [EMP003][EMP003] | Fictional ice-cream names differing in a vowel altered richness/creaminess judgments and evaluation. | Laboratory/student setting; first study n=126. Naming-information diagnosticity mattered; judgments and intentions were not actual beverage sales. |
| Sound-category fit — Lowrey & Shrum (2007) [EMP004][EMP004] | Two experiments found that vowel preferences depended on desirable category attributes. | Fictitious paired names and stated preference. A sound can fit one attribute and conflict with another; no universally superior vowel follows. |
| Cross-language sound symbolism — Shrum et al. (2012) [EMP005][EMP005] | Category-dependent vowel patterns appeared among French-, Spanish- and Chinese-speaking English bilinguals. | Samples 106/88/181; vehicles/tools and artificial names, not Czech beverage buyers. This does not validate emotion translations or all EU languages. |
| Pronunciation fluency — Laham, Koval & Alter (2012) [EMP006][EMP006] | Five studies linked pronunciation ease with more positive impressions. | Personal names/people, with experimental and observational components. Indirect support for fluency testing, not a beverage revenue effect. |
| Suggestiveness and message recall — Keller, Heckler & Houston (1998) [EMP007][EMP007] | A benefit-suggestive name improved congruent-benefit recall but reduced later unrelated-benefit recall. | Abstract; television-brand advertising experiment. Specificity can guide attention, but this is not proof every narrow name prevents extension. |
| Length — Rightsizing brand names (2026) [EMP008][EMP008], [EMP009][EMP009] | Longer non-word names improved tested recognition; attitude had a different nonlinear pattern. | n=98 people, 36 fictional car/fridge/phone names; abstract plus author brief of the same study. Forced recognition differs from unaided recall; no short-or-long universal rule. |
| Extensions — Aaker & Keller (1990) [EMP010][EMP010] | Perceived fit and parent-brand quality contributed to extension judgments. | Publisher abstract; learned brand meaning and product compatibility matter, not just the breadth of the word. No guarantee of successful diversification. |

The practical inference is to optimise several tasks, not one aesthetic impression. **Recall** asks someone to generate a name; **recognition** asks them to identify it when shown; **retrieval** asks them to recover the correct brand after imperfect hearing or spelling. One task's advantage can be another's cost. Initial familiarity can help pronunciation while making the word harder to own mentally among similar names. Unusual spelling can distinguish a visual memory but lose a podcast listener.

Sound symbolism is a tool for generating hypotheses about expected size, texture, intensity or tone. It is not a biochemical mechanism and cannot demonstrate that a drink relaxes the buyer. Even genuine phonetic effects may depend on language, product attributes, familiarity, exposure and the question asked. We found no basis to prescribe a universal Czech/EU “calming vowel”, consonant cluster, maximum syllable count, rhythm/alliteration pattern or naming-to-sales uplift. Treat rhythm, repeated sounds and hesitation as observations in read-aloud tests, not a formula for success.

### Practitioner practice versus empirical evidence

Lexicon describes combining creative development with linguistic/cultural assessment, customer research and trademark evaluation. That is a useful workflow; its commercial success list is not causal validation of its method. Kantar describes brand recognition through a system of packs, colours, logos and other cues. Its diagnostic framework helps formulate tests, but is not an independent estimate of the name's isolated contribution. [LEAD028][LEAD028], [LEAD029][LEAD029]

Claims such as “all great names have two syllables”, “a hard consonant always sells”, “an available .com means the name is ownable”, “an invented word has no baggage” and “a broad name guarantees extensions” are unsupported as universal rules. An evocative name's story is not evidence that an unbriefed buyer will derive the same meaning. Nor is retrospective familiarity evidence that a new entrant can afford the same ambiguity.

## 4. Naming approaches and their trade-offs

The types overlap. We assign one primary code for counting and keep secondary codes and origin confidence. A dictionary meaning, a founder's documented rationale and a researcher's association are different facts.

| Approach | Potential contribution | Cost or boundary | Illustrations / interpretation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Descriptive | Immediate category/offer understanding and useful listing words. | Weak distinctiveness for the described goods; can constrain category/price; still needs identity. | Dollar Shave Club; “drinks” in Ugly Drinks does some category work. |
| Benefit-led | Makes the desired outcome salient. | Strong expectation, substantiation and claims burden; benefit may be too narrow. | Functional family names often carry this job; CELSIUS indirectly suggests a scientific benefit context rather than naming a literal ingredient. |
| Suggestive/associative | Gives a relevant clue without a complete description. | Interpretation may vary or be missed; weak suggestions can look generic. | innocent, Glossier; their associations are not universal or legally cleared. |
| Arbitrary real word | Familiar vocabulary can acquire a distinctive category meaning. | Ordinary search competition and initial category opacity. | Apple; a common word's legal distinctiveness depends on the goods. |
| Invented/altered spelling | Potentially distinctive lexical identity and flexible learned meaning. | Audio-to-spelling friction, false friends and similar existing marks still possible. | Pepsi, ZICO, Birell; distinct origins and business histories. |
| Compound | Combines cues, contrast or rhythm. | Length, spacing and ambiguity; merely combining descriptive terms may remain descriptive. | Coca-Cola, Liquid Death, Club-Mate; multiple mechanisms, not a single style. |
| Founder/personal | Human authorship and a narrative anchor. | Person/reputation dependence, rights questions and pronunciation. | IKEA contains founder/place initials; Warby Parker's literary names are not founders. A personal-sounding name must not be presented as founder provenance without evidence. |
| Place-based | Landscape, origin, craft or cultural specificity. | False provenance, language and protected-name concerns; expansion can retain or contradict the origin story. | Patagonia and Pentire; place meaning differs from legal geographical protection. |
| Emotional/occasion | Names an invitation, ritual, state or moment rather than an active ingredient. | Crowding, abstract category and implied physiological promise. | Recess, Daytrip, TÖST; social occasion and mood alteration must be distinguished. |
| Provocative/humorous | Creates an unusual contrast and a repeatable verbal voice. | Can repel, confuse, reduce appetite or demand expensive continuous creative execution. | Liquid Death and discontinued Ugly Drinks show why provocation is not sufficient. |
| Ingredient-led | Anchors credibility, expertise and category expectation. | Reformulation and non-ingredient extension become harder. | Oatly; OLIPOP's documented ingredient root may be invisible to ordinary buyers. |
| Metaphor/cultural reference | Compresses imagery or values into a recognisable idea. | Reference may be unfamiliar, culturally different or legally crowded. | Nike, Red Bull, Monster, Sierra Mist; pleasing and aggressive metaphors both occur. |

No broad-name preference is absolute. Oatly can unite many oat-based uses through specificity. A new firm committed to one enduring ingredient might benefit from that focus. Conversely, tying this undecided product to magnesium, CBD, sleep or focus would impose a promise before the team knows it can or should keep it.

### What the category comparisons show

**Soda and functional soda often put familiarity into the product, not solely the parent.** Cola, fruit flavours, soda/water descriptors and familiar pack formats explain the drink while the name distinguishes the maker. Pop-like language can connect to refreshment but also produces a crowded naming neighbourhood. OLIPOP, poppi and Culture Pop cannot establish that “pop” caused growth; the similarity also raises confusion and legal-search questions.

**Social and alcohol-alternative names often express ritual, identity or a feeling.** Recess, Hiyo, Kin Euphorics, Ghia, De Soi and TÖST do different jobs: break, belonging, social/experiential language, premium identity or toast-like ritual. Some require an explanatory descriptor. These are analyst readings unless an origin is specifically documented. The desired feeling may be communicated by pack, imagery and copy rather than derivable from the name itself. Commercial scale is uneven and often private. [Beverage source-linked analysis](supporting/workstreams/beverage.md)

**Scientific/ingredient language appears at multiple levels.** Oatly is visibly ingredient-led in English; CELSIUS uses a scientific term; OLIPOP has a documented ingredient-related coinage but does not plainly say fibre to a first-time reader. Functional families can be more explicit than the masterbrand. This can preserve the parent while making variants comprehensible, but creates country-specific claims dependencies. [LEAD007][LEAD007], [LEAD017][LEAD017], [BEV025][BEV025]

**Unexpected names need a coherent explanation system.** Liquid Death's category labels and consistently themed product names explain water, tea and energy under one voice. The contradiction is part of a broader marketing/product system, not a recommendation to use frightening vocabulary. Ugly Drinks demonstrates that a provocative everyday word plus a descriptor is compatible with discontinuation too. [LEAD009][LEAD009], [BEV052][BEV052]

**Local and European examples do not converge on one linguistic style.** Kofola and Birell are coined identities; CANS uses an English packaging noun; PRAGER'S uses a name/place-like form; Vilgain is a broader wellness identity; Club-Mate and fritz-kola retain category/ingredient language. UK brands provide additional inspiration but are not EU compliance precedents. None establishes Czech preference for English, invented words or heritage. Exact origins and unknowns are in the dataset.

**The same broad mechanisms cross outcomes.** Large-scale CELSIUS and discontinued Sierra Mist both rely on borrowed meaning rather than a literal recipe. Liquid Death and Ugly Drinks both use unexpected language. Short ZICO survived multiple ownership/status changes. Dollar Shave Club achieved scale but later generated an impairment and ownership change; Casper's historical losses do not make its current offer nonexistent. These are counterexamples to folklore, not matched performance estimates. ## 6. Brand, product, function and flavour are different naming jobs

| Layer | Main job | Example or proposed use |
|---|---|---|
| Legal company | Contracts, ownership and corporate identity. | PepsiCo is not the same thing as Pepsi; corporate portfolio breadth is not a Pepsi masterbrand extension. [LEAD003][LEAD003] |
| Masterbrand | Stable source identity and accumulated associations. | Recess, Red Bull, Liquid Death. |
| Product family | Distinguish materially different drink propositions when necessary. | Recess Mood versus original; Monster Ultra/Java/Rehab; optional for a one-formula startup. |
| Functional variant | Signal actual composition/claim differences. | A legally reviewed family or nutrient statement; not automatic permission to say “Calm” or “Focus”. |
| Flavour | Help selection and repeat ordering. | Plain fruit names, or themed names accompanied by intelligible taste information. |
| Category descriptor | Explain what the product is. | Sparkling water, cola, nonalcoholic aperitif, oat drink; local lawful name assessed separately. |
| Tagline/promise | Express voice, occasion or a supportable benefit. | Liquid Death's thirst rhetoric is distinct from its literal category descriptions. |

Observed architectures offer useful alternatives:

- **One masterbrand, one formula, several flavours:** lowest naming complexity. Keep a stable parent and readable flavours. Do not add a vague functional family merely to look sophisticated.
- **One masterbrand, several clearly named families:** Recess shows a parent spanning differentiated ranges; Monster separates product forms/uses with families. This can reuse recognition, but parent associations may spill into a new line, and strong family promises still need review. [BEV005][BEV005], [LEAD005][LEAD005]
- **Ingredient platform across categories:** Oatly keeps a common ingredient through drinks and foods. That is flexible within an ingredient system, potentially restrictive outside it. [LEAD017][LEAD017]
- **Attitude platform across beverages:** Liquid Death lists water, sparkling products, tea and energy under a common voice. Its category labels carry much of the literal explanation. [LEAD009][LEAD009]
- **Separate consumer brands under one company:** Kofola's corporate portfolio and PepsiCo show how a company can expand without forcing every category under one parent. This costs more separate identity-building and is not a launch recommendation. [LEAD003][LEAD003], [LEAD012][LEAD012]

Name change and repositioning examples need careful interpretation. Mother Beverage to poppi involved broader rebranding; Sierra Mist to Starry involved recipe and marketing changes. De Soi's Purple Lune to Vino Violette is a variant-level change rather than a different company. Coca-Cola Original Taste/Zero Sugar demonstrates explicit variant descriptors within a durable parent. None isolates an incremental naming effect. [BEV008][BEV008], [BEV029][BEV029], [LEAD024][LEAD024], [LEAD025][LEAD025]

For this project, prototype **[masterbrand] + [optional family] + [flavour] + [truthful local category]**. Stress-test two flavours, two different benefit families, a social-versus-functional pair and a realistic ingredient change, and removal/localisation of a functional statement for a different country. The parent must remain recognisable while mandatory information and permissible claims adapt locally. A broad umbrella may technically fit everything and still mean little to anyone; choose actual expansion needs over imaginary optionality. Optional CBD should remain a clearly separate future decision, not determine the core identity.

## 7. DTC, spoken discovery and the retail shelf

These are operational hypotheses and test requirements unless measured evidence is cited; no conversion uplift is inferred from visual appeal or engagement.

| Context | What the name must help people do | Practical test and supporting role |
|---|---|---|
| Spoken recommendation, podcast, creator | Hear once, retain a useful sound and recover the correct spelling/brand. | Audio-to-text and realistic query task; test without showing the logo or teaching spelling. |
| Search results | Distinguish brand from dictionary meanings, people and unrelated companies. | Exact/near spelling and name+drink queries by country/language; date the audit. Recess, Away and CELSIUS illustrate lexical ambiguity, not measured poor SEO. |
| Social handles | Connect mention and account consistently. | Say handle aloud; check truncation, punctuation and confusing variants. Exact handle symmetry is useful, not a universal hard requirement. |
| Online listings | Recognise parent, category, flavour, quantity and relevant composition. | Mobile listing identification with identical layout; put literal information in product title/descriptor instead of overloading the masterbrand. |
| Subscription purchase | Reorder the correct product across variants. | Identify next delivery's formula/flavour; observe future paid repeat separately. A subscription offer says nothing about retention. |
| Crowded shelf | Locate the brand and understand which drink it is quickly. | Matched shelf simulation, real-scale pack and controlled position; measure mistakes and findability, not likes. |
| Café or bar | Order and repeat the name under ordinary speech/noise. | Role-play with native speakers, ordinary articles/cases and “one [name]”; record correction requests. |
| Small can/bottle | Preserve legibility of parent, flavour and descriptor. | Real-size mockup; colour-blind/colour-independent variant check; long names need different layout, not automatic rejection. |

Google's current guidance recommends unique, concise, consistent site names and warns that generic names may not be selected as a site's displayed name. This concerns search-result presentation, not a promise that invented names rank higher or that keywords ensure discovery. Good metadata and consistent usage cannot compensate for a confusing spoken name in every channel. [LEAD027][LEAD027]

Abstract names can work because colour, wordmark, product image, price, shelf placement and descriptor supply missing information. A logo that makes an unusual spelling recognisable visually does not necessarily make it recoverable by ear. Likewise an online landing page has more room to explain “social tonic” than a bar menu or small can. Test first impressions with and without the descriptor; do not translate explained comprehension into unaided understanding.

DTC-origin is not a synonym for any brand with an online shop. The data distinguish documented origins from current direct ordering and unknown channel histories. Warby Parker, Casper and Dollar Shave Club offer channel-transition lessons, while Pepsi, Kofola and innocent have different founding paths. Broad retail scale entails distribution and shelf support that a new online seller does not inherit. [CON011][CON011], [CON013][CON013], [CON017][CON017], [LEAD002][LEAD002], [LEAD012][LEAD012], [LEAD015][LEAD015]

## 8. Czech and EU language requirements

Language facts below come from linguistic/teaching references. Their naming implications are **desk hypotheses**, not native-speaker or representative consumer validation. The proposed diagnostic set is Czech first, Slovak for nearby expansion, German and Polish for neighbouring markets, and French for a contrasting Romance-language check. It is not a ranking of launch countries or a substitute for the rest of the EU. English familiarity must be measured in the intended buyer group.

| Language/context | Supported fact | Naming test implied |
|---|---|---|
| Czech | Foreign names adapt in stress and sound; readings can vary. [LEG001][LEG001] | Read aloud without instruction; record stable local variants instead of demanding English pronunciation. |
| Czech grammar | Inflection depends on gender and spoken ending; some names remain indeclinable. [LEG002][LEG002] | Test “Dám si…”, “Máš ještě…?”, “Bez…”, “Ochutnal/a jsem…”, plus spontaneous abbreviations. Indeclinable is not inherently bad. |
| Slovak | Stress is usually initial. [LEG003][LEG003] | Separate meaning and heard-to-written checks; Czech similarity does not guarantee transfer. |
| German | Stress patterns differ for stems, prefixes, compounds and foreign-word endings. [LEG004][LEG004] | Test segmentation, stress, spelling and unintended compounds in Germany/Austria as relevant. |
| Polish | Penultimate stress is common, with exceptions. [LEG005][LEG005] | Three-syllable candidates can gain a different rhythm; check spelling and local meanings independently. |
| French | Tonic accent operates at the end of a syntactic group. [LEG006][LEG006] | Test in phrases as well as isolation, including sound-to-spelling and final-letter handling. The reference is Canadian linguistic guidance, not French consumer evidence. |

Diacritics can preserve local meaning and identity; removal can alter pronunciation and create collisions. They should not be rejected automatically. Current .cz registration rules use a–z, digits and hyphens, so a diacritic-bearing Czech brand needs a usable domain spelling. This domain rule is not a rule for every European platform or register. [LEG007][LEG007]

Potentially troublesome combinations include English silent letters, **th**, **w**, vowel sequences, double consonants that are not audible, and accent-free versions of accented words. These are screening prompts, not proof of failure. TÖST and De Soi deserve a different audio test from Kofola; the fact that all can be printed on a can says nothing about equal spoken usability. Avoid unverified jokes about foreign mistranslations. Check dictionaries, colloquial usage and native explanations for each actual candidate before making a claim.

“Social tonic” and “functional soda” should be treated as terms to explain and test, not assumed Czech categories. A tonic may suggest a mixer or ingredient; a social label may suggest alcohol replacement, sociability or an effect. “Functional” does not specify the function. Start with an accurate local drink description, ask what buyers infer, then test the occasion/benefit separately. Translate the intended communication job, not just the English phrase. Emotional and premium connotations can change across language groups. [Language memo](supporting/workstreams/language-legal.md)

## 9. Trademark and claims framework

This is **not clearance**. No sampled name, proposed territory or future candidate is declared available. Trademark distinctiveness, consumer distinctiveness and commercial discoverability are different dimensions.

A descriptive/generic sign may fail as a source identifier for the relevant goods; an ordinary word can be distinctive for unrelated goods. An invented spelling does not automatically fix descriptive meaning or a similar sound. EUIPO considers visual, phonetic and conceptual resemblance in context. An EU mark may face refusal even if the issue exists only in part of the EU, so a Czech/English-only linguistic screen cannot complete an EU legal assessment. [LEG008][LEG008], [LEG009][LEG009], [LEG010][LEG010], [LEG011][LEG011]

Start a future search with the exact product and territories. WIPO's 2026 Nice classification points commonly to **class 32** for nonalcoholic drinks/beer, with **30** for coffee/tea/cocoa-based drinks and **29** for milk-predominating drinks/milk substitutes. **5** concerns supplements and medical-purpose goods where applicable; functional branding does not automatically make an ordinary drink a supplement. **33** alcoholic drinks other than beer and **35** relevant retail/other services may be adjacent search considerations. A class number is not a safe harbour: same class does not alone establish similarity, and different classes do not establish dissimilarity. [LEG010][LEG010], [LEG012][LEG012]

Use Czech ÚPV, EUIPO/TMview and WIPO searches for exact names, stems, sound-alikes, translations where relevant, diacritic-free forms and adjacent goods. Review territory, status, priority, owner, goods and reputation; obtain qualified review of actual candidates. A company registration, free domain or available handle is a different record and does not establish trademark rights. UK protection requires its own territorial plan. [LEG013][LEG013], [LEG014][LEG014], [LEG015][LEG015], [LEG016][LEG016]

Food law applies to names too. Regulation 1924/2006 Article 1(3) addresses trademarks/brand/fancy names understood as nutrition or health claims; a related compliant claim is required. General health-related wellbeing references under Article 10(3) need a specific authorised claim. An unrelated vitamin statement does not legitimise a stronger calm or cognitive promise. Food information must not mislead or attribute disease treatment, prevention or cure; a fancy name does not replace the lawful name of the food. [LEG017][LEG017], [LEG018][LEG018], [LEG024][LEG024]

Review the complete name, descriptor, images and copy. “Calm”, “focus”, “sleep”, “energy”, “gut-friendly” or “no crash” may imply physiological claims; they are not a universal banned-word list. A genuine serving occasion is different from a claim of altered health. Recent CJEU decisions require contextual and transition-specific analysis, especially for botanicals; “on hold” is not blanket permission. [LEG019][LEG019], [LEG022][LEG022], [LEG023][LEG023]

Czech alcohol wording matters for a potential “zero” identity. SZPI distinguishes nonalcoholic drinks (up to 0.5% ABV) from voluntary zero/no-alcohol representations interpreted as no alcohol. Its discussion of 0.05% concerns analytical quantification, not a separate legal allowance. Do not generalise this guidance to every EU country/category. Likewise “natural/clean” wording requires contextual substantiation; neither unrestricted permission nor a universal ban is justified. [LEG020][LEG020], [LEG021][LEG021]

The core must stand independently of CBD. A later CBD variant needs its own ingredient/use and claims route, which a trademark, UK sale or US benchmark cannot establish. This report does not clear any CBD beverage. [LEG025][LEG025]

## 10. Project synthesis and next brief

The most defensible principles are: test processing and memory in the actual language/channel; preserve a useful semantic cue without overclaiming; distinguish lexical novelty from recognition earned through exposure; evaluate the name with a stable descriptor/pack; and keep commercial evidence separate from causal naming claims.

The practical territories are **shared moment/transition; connection/welcoming identity; sensory/natural-world metaphor; approachable coinage; unexpected everyday word/juxtaposition; and credible craft/place/personal signature**. These are strategic directions, not name candidates or a preferred final direction. The [brief](supporting/naming-brief.md) explains communication potential, product compatibility, precedents, confusion/overpromising risks and required language/legal validation for each.

| Territory | What it could communicate and accommodate | Research basis / precedent | Risks | Required language and legal validation |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **A shared moment or transition** | An invitation to pause, gather or mark the end of one activity; potentially social and work-break families. | Recess and Daytrip show occasion/movement language; context-dependent semantic retrieval findings support relevant cues, not these specific names. | Crowded pause/reset language; a school break may feel childish; a transition may imply an immediate mood effect. | Unaided Czech interpretations; whether the intended moment survives German/Polish/French; similar names; health implications in full pack context. |
| **Connection and welcoming identity** | Participation, conviviality and a shared table; social drink first, potentially broader refreshments. | Hiyo/Kin/TÖST illustrate belonging and ritual cues; these are observed positioning choices, not proven sales mechanisms. | Generic “together” language; forced sociability; implication of confidence, disinhibition or intoxication. | Natural bar-order usage; inclusion without moralising about alcohol; slang; phonetic/semantic mark conflicts; implied-effect review. |
| **A sensory or natural-world metaphor** | Texture, brightness, atmosphere, landscape or movement; adaptable to flavours and different moments. | Sierra Mist, Red Bull, Pentire and Patagonia illustrate varied metaphor/place mechanisms and very different outcomes. | Generic botanical sameness, imagined provenance, organic/natural inference, geographically narrow credibility. | Literal translations, protected geographical references where relevant, truthful origin/ingredient expectations, bilingual associations. |
| **An approachable coined word** | A distinctive identity whose meaning can be built through experience; broad social and functional product families. | Pepsi, Hiyo, ZICO and OLIPOP demonstrate different coinage routes. Short invented names also occur in failed/relaunched businesses. | Spelling from audio; sound-alikes; hidden claim roots; absence of initial category meaning; crowded trademark space. | Pronunciation and audio transcription in each market, search confusion, variant spellings, full legal similarity search. No automatic two-syllable preference. |
| **An unexpected everyday word or juxtaposition** | A memorable point of view and recognisable voice; suitable where brand entertainment is part of the proposition. | Apple/Away and Liquid Death show ordinary vocabulary used outside literal category expectations. | Ordinary-word search competition; humour fails or overwhelms appetite; inappropriate death/drug/sedation cues; similarity to category rebels. | Spontaneous associations before product reveal; equivalent packaging; creator and café speech tests; confusing existing marks. Explore the mechanism, not imitation of the precedents. |
| **Credible craft, place or personal signature** | Care, recipe authorship or local origin; taste-led premium positioning with multiple flavours. | Founder/personal and place precedents across the sample; exact origins documented only where sources support them. | Founder dependence, invented heritage, possessive punctuation, origin expectations, difficult Czech or foreign inflection. | Verify factual story and origin; identify who owns relevant personal rights; local pronunciation and naming conventions; geographic and trademark review. |

A direct-benefit or ingredient-led challenger should remain eligible if positioning and the durable formula justify it. Broadness is a trade-off, not a virtue by itself. For now, avoid forcing one ingredient or effect into the parent while the product remains undecided. Choose the first meaningful customer promise before choosing the final identity.

### Evaluation framework and neutral test design

Use hard holds for unacceptable trademark/confusion risk, false or unlawful claims, serious local-language problems and incompatibility with the actual product. Unknowns remain pending. Do not let a high aesthetic score override a hard issue.

For viable candidates, use an evidence profile—not a sales-prediction score—covering distinctiveness, recall/recognition, pronunciation, spelling/spoken search, associations, category comprehension, positioning fit, range flexibility, cross-language usability and trademark/claims risk. Record weak/workable/strong/unknown with evidence, group, denominator, uncertainty and potential remedy. Liking comes last.

The proposed protocol separates visual reading from audio spelling; elicits unaided associations and product expectations before revealing the drink; measures delayed recall before recognition; then compares matched packaging and descriptors. Randomise order, balance exposure, use plausible foils, include no-answer/no-choice options and analyse languages separately. Only first exposures are unprimed. A separate brief-exposure memory arm avoids contaminating recall with earlier association questions; otherwise report recall as following elaborated exposure. Same colour, type, pack and price prevent a favourite design from masquerading as a favourite name. Search tests must distinguish live collision audits from mock prelaunch retrieval tasks. No participant has been recruited and no results exist. The full [protocol](supporting/evaluation-and-testing.md) contains neutral prompts, planning limits and a decision record.

## 11. Material limitations

This is a purposive public-source desk review, not a systematic meta-analysis, market census or matched causal study. Private commercial numbers are uneven, some source pages are partial, and the weak-outcome sample is small. Historical figures are labelled and do not become 2026 run rates. Company sources are valuable for offers and stated intent, less independent for performance claims. No raw retail panel, sales cohort, search-volume or customer-conversion data was purchased or inspected.

Semantic codes, tone and category independence are analyst judgments; they are not consumer ratings. Word counts use whitespace tokens (hyphenated names remain one token); characters include spaces/punctuation; syllables are indicative for the stated reading and blank where uncertain. Different coding conventions could alter counts. Brand origins are explicitly marked unverified where not documented; missing etymology is preferable to an invented story.

The language coverage is diagnostic, not EU-wide; no native-speaker validation, participant testing, trademark similarity clearance or product-label approval occurred. Naming cannot validate formulation, efficacy, repeat purchase or profitable distribution. Independent review challenged evidence and methods; it does not supply independent market observations. Details and corrections are retained in [methods and limitations](supporting/methods-and-limitations.md) and the [review record](supporting/independent-review.md).
