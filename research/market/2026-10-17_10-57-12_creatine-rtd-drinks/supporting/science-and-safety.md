# Human science and safety: relevance to creatine soda

**17 October 2026 · Europe/Prague, UTC+02:00.** This is an evidence review, not consumption advice or product clearance. Study doses below describe experiments; they are not safe formulation recommendations. See [the technical review](formulation-and-stability.md), [studies](../data/studies-workstream.csv), [technical references](technical-sources.csv) and the assignment's separate EU/Czech legal review.

## Established physiological proposition and its practical limit

Creatine has a defensible repeated-use sports proposition. EFSA's [foods-for-sportspeople assessment](https://www.efsa.europa.eu/en/supporting/pub/en-871), T025, recognises effects on repeated, short, high-intensity activity. That does not mean one occasional soda creates a noticeable immediate lift, improves every endurance task or rebuilds muscle without a training stimulus. Ingredient evidence also does not certify the contents or efficacy of a stored beverage.

The primary evidence makes timing important. In [Hultman et al., 1996](https://journals.physiology.org/doi/abs/10.1152/jappl.1996.81.1.232), T014, 31 men received different regimens: muscle creatine rose approximately 20% after 20 g/day for six days, or more gradually over 28 days at 3 g/day. This measured tissue accumulation, not a promise that every consumer feels a benefit after precisely four weeks. A daily ritual is therefore a plausible adherence proposition; proof that soda improves adherence over powder is still missing.

[Volek et al., 1999](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10449017/), T015, randomised 19 resistance-trained men to capsules or placebo during 12 weeks of heavy training. The protocol used 25 g/day for one week then 5 g/day; strength and muscle-fibre outcomes favoured creatine. The small male athletic sample and high initial regimen do not establish effects of an occasional can in the general population.

A useful contrary result is [the 2025 lean-body-mass trial](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11944689/), T023: 63 adults were randomised to 5 g/day monohydrate or no supplement, with a one-week wash-in followed by 12 weeks of resistance training. Lean mass rose during wash-in, but later gains did not differ between groups. The absence of a placebo and participant blinding limits inference; the paper describes the trial as single blind; nonetheless, lean mass, water changes and actual muscle hypertrophy should not be marketed as identical endpoints. This does not erase the wider sports evidence.

## Cognitive and everyday-energy promises remain a weaker basis

| Primary or authoritative evidence | Finding relevant to a beverage claim | Limitation |
|---|---|---|
| [Sandkühler et al., 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37968687/), T016 | Preregistered crossover, 123 analysed participants (148 randomised), 5 g/day for six weeks per period. Main frequentist tests did not reach significance; Bayesian analysis was compatible with a small benefit. No exploratory-test improvement; more reported side effects with creatine. | Not evidence of a reliable immediate mental boost; healthy adults, not a finished drink. |
| [Gordji-Nejad et al., 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38418482/), T017 | Small crossover sleep-deprivation experiment used a single 0.35 g/kg monohydrate dose, with cognitive/MRS measures over hours; reported improvements under that stress. | Approximately 24.5 g for a 70 kg participant is arithmetic, not a recommended dose. Specific sleep deprivation and a much larger dose do not substantiate a normal 3–5 g soda. |
| [EFSA, 2024](https://efsa.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.2903/j.efsa.2024.9100), T018 | Assessment of a proposed adult cognitive-function claim found that the submitted evidence did not establish cause and effect. | An authoritative assessment, not a declaration that every possible cognitive effect is impossible. |
| [2026 Wingate/cognition trial](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41960895/), T024 | Forty active omnivorous men/women; one week of 20 g/day monohydrate or placebo. Indexed primary report title/results describe improved cycling Wingate performance without cognitive improvement. | Abstract/indexed record accessed; publisher full text blocked. No effect sizes extracted or beverage transfer claimed. |

EU legal status is now more decisive than an older review alone: [Commission Regulation (EU) 2026/1118](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX:32026R1118), T027, refuses the proposed cognitive claim. Consult the integrated legal review for the exact current implications. A clinical or mechanistic hypothesis is not a permitted EU marketing claim.

Creia correctly describes accumulation rather than an instant stimulant effect, but its exact staged percentages, broad workday benefits and lower-dose absorption statement are not verified drink outcomes. The [CreaBev human trial](https://assets.cureus.com/uploads/original_article/pdf/83527/20220523-15441-1ndk31g.pdf) used fresh powder and found no significant pharmacokinetic superiority over ordinary monohydrate (T010). Brand and ingredient evidence must remain separate.

## Safety and repeat consumption

Healthy-adult monohydrate trials are generally reassuring within their tested conditions. In [Vilar Neto et al., 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32670557/), T019, 36 healthy male students were recruited for placebo or 3/5 g/day over 35 days. Kidney-injury markers did not differ between groups. Serum creatinine increased within supplemented groups, potentially affecting creatinine-derived eGFR without demonstrating kidney injury. This is a reason for accurate clinical interpretation, not a basis to dismiss an individual's abnormal results.

[Gualano et al., 2008](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18188581/), T020, studied 18 healthy men with exercise training and approximately 10 g/day over three months; cystatin-C findings did not show impairment. Neither small trial establishes unlimited-dose, lifelong, all-population or aged-beverage safety.

Dose pattern can matter for comfort. [Ostojic & Ahmetovic, 2008](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18373286/), T021, randomised 59 male soccer players to 2×5 g/day, 1×10 g/day or placebo for 28 days. Reported diarrhoea was more common with the single 10 g dose than split dosing or placebo. This does not establish that any particular soda causes diarrhoea; it argues against treating several high-dose cans as ordinary unrestricted refreshment.

Safety assessments are not interchangeable with efficacy thresholds or legal maximums. [AESAN's 2024 assessment](https://efsa.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.2903/fr.efsa.2024.FR-0046), T022, found 3 g/day creatine equivalent acceptable for healthy-adult supplements and explicitly distinguished compound masses. The older [SCF 2000 assessment](https://food.ec.europa.eu/system/files/2020-12/sci-com_scf_out70_en.pdf), T026, was conservative about high loading doses. These are not proof that all higher research doses are unsafe, nor approval for an ordinary beverage in every EU country.

A product-specific exposure assessment should account for one can, multiple cans, powder/capsules already used, background intake, initial overage and the highest justified serving pattern. It must separately evaluate caffeine, electrolytes, sugars/sweeteners and all carriers. A caffeine-free version avoids adding caffeine exposure but does not resolve creatine-dose or degradation questions.

Evidence from selected healthy adults should not be transferred automatically to children, pregnancy/lactation, impaired kidney function or people using relevant medicines. A qualified safety review should define target and excluded populations, appropriate information and cumulative exposure. This review does not determine a universally safe dose or prescribe a warning text.

Creatinine is a degradation product and a clinical biomarker; detecting it in a beverage is not by itself proof that the drink is nephrotoxic. Equally, loss of intact creatine should not be dismissed because creatinine is naturally present in the body. The amount and profile of degradants, exposure, relevant toxicology and label accuracy require assessment. No safe beverage creatinine limit or valid overage was established here.

## What could support a credible product proposition

A potential proposition is an enjoyable portable way for an informed adult to maintain a scientifically relevant intake, with the dose verified through expiry. Taste, adherence, willingness to pay and repeated consumption of the exact formula remain commercial hypotheses. They need comparison against inexpensive freshly mixed powder and an ordinary beverage, not against no available alternative.

Broad statements about immediate mental clarity, all-day energy, no bloating, superior absorption or putting back age-related muscle loss should not be adopted from US product pages. The evidence and authorised wording are narrower. No study located here demonstrates that Creia or another currently sold creatine soda improves adherence, cognitive performance, training outcomes or safety relative to matched-dose powder.

## Method and access limits

Targeted searches combined creatine/monohydrate/HCl/citrate/CreaBev with stability, pH, temperature, HPLC, creatinine, pharmacokinetics, cognition, exercise and renal safety. Primary publication records, original PDFs, institutional repositories and authoritative assessments took priority. Full texts were checked when accessible; T002/T004/T005/T006/T024 have explicit abstract or preview limits. Review and supplier references were traced to originals for decisive numbers. This is a focused critical review, not a registered systematic review, pooled efficacy estimate or exhaustive toxicology dossier.

The 2020 conference summaries and 2021 CreaBev publication appear related and were not treated as independent replication. The 2022 pharmacokinetic study, older supplier rat claims and brand marketing also are not three independent proofs of human superiority. No company was contacted and no experiments, purchases or commissioned work occurred.
