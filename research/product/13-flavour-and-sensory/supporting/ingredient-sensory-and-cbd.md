# Ingredient sensory risks and separate CBD gate

**Desk research · 17 October 2026 · Czech/EU product decision; international ingredient evidence.** No ingredients or finished products were tasted, purchased or commissioned. This is a material-selection and experiment plan, not a recipe, safety clearance or approved label. Finished launch formats remain ready-to-drink liquid cans/bottles. Raw powders discussed below are manufacturing ingredients, not consumer-format benchmarks.

Read with the [ingredient risk register](../data/ingredient-risk-register.csv), [source ledger](ingredient-sources.csv), the existing [EU/scientific memo](../../../market/01-us-product-landscape/supporting/eu-czech-and-evidence.md) and [updated EU/Czech evidence](../../../market/04-eu-czech-competition/supporting/regulatory-and-evidence-update.md). Those reports establish why commercial popularity, ingredient effects, food-use permission and health claims must remain separate. This work adds material-specific sensory evidence; it does not reclassify any existing formula as launch-ready.

## What the evidence actually supports

### Focus and energy

**Caffeine:** human sensory work tested caffeine at 1.5, 3 and 4.5 mM with selected salts, sucrose, milk and aromas. Its bitterness could be partially modified, with effects dependent on the combination; this supports a controlled masking experiment, not a universal masking ingredient. The concentrations and matrices in the study are not proposed beverage targets. [IN01: Keast, 2008](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0950329308000207).

Start by comparing the same fruit/tea base with and without the exact caffeine preparation at the intended finished concentration, once a competent formulator has established an appropriate formulation and exposure plan. Keep sweetness, acid, aroma and carbonation fixed. Measure bitter intensity after swallowing and at 30–60 seconds, full-serving liking, and late-serving rejection. A tea-derived caffeine ingredient needs a specification for accompanying solids and polyphenols; “natural caffeine” alone does not specify its flavour. Do not make an aperitif's deliberately lingering bitterness the standard for office focus.

**L-theanine:** a small Japanese human study of pure L-theanine found a complex concentration-dependent taste and umami synergy with IMP. A separate study found sourness reduction associated with both pH change and umami in its model solutions. Neither proves that a practical RTD concentration masks caffeine or tastes neutral. [IN02: Narukawa et al., 2008](https://doi.org/10.1271/bbb.80328); [IN03: Narukawa et al., 2010](https://www.jstage.jst.go.jp/article/fstr/16/5/16_5_487/_article/-char/en/).

A theanine addition therefore needs its own blinded comparison, including pH and titratable acidity. Match pH in a second comparison if testing a perceptual masking mechanism; otherwise acidity changes can be mistaken for intrinsic taste improvement. Exact source, purity, production route and ordinary-beverage food-use status remain supplier/regulatory gates. Ingredient efficacy evidence reviewed in topic 01 does not establish a no-crash or sustained-productivity claim for a can.

**Tea:** human sensory evaluation of EC, ECG, EGC and EGCG found concentration-dependent taste intensity, predominantly bitterness and astringency; ECG and EGCG were not interchangeable in their profiles. This is evidence about specific catechins in model solutions, not proof that all green-tea extracts taste alike. [IN04: Narukawa et al., 2010, Japan](https://academic.oup.com/ijfst/article/45/8/1579/7865323).

Specify leaf/infusion or extract, catechin profile, caffeine, soluble solids, carriers and extraction conditions. Assess drying/puckering separately from bitter taste. Hypotheses worth testing are a lighter tea load, a less astringent tea preparation, or fruit aroma plus modest sweetness; none is a verified solution in the proposed product. Include still tea as well as lightly sparkling fruit-led focus drinks. Match active exposure when comparing bases, or clearly label the confounding difference.

### Hydration and mineral-containing calm drinks

**The salt matters.** Human evidence documents bitter, salty and other sensations from magnesium chloride and magnesium sulfate in the tested aqueous systems. The same paper's organic-anion comparison was of **calcium** salts. It cannot justify the assertion that magnesium lactate is proven less bitter than magnesium citrate. [IN05: Lawless et al., 2003](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0950329302001283).

Two more relevant sourcing candidates are:

- **Jungbunzlauer magnesium lactate dihydrate**, CAS 18917-93-6: supplier describes neutral taste, approximately 10% magnesium and water solubility of 84 g/L. These are manufacturer specifications/claims, not independent panel results in our drink. [IN06, undated](https://www.jungbunzlauer.com/ingredients/magnesium-lactate/).
- **Jungbunzlauer trimagnesium citrate**, anhydrous CAS 3344-18-1 or nonahydrate CAS 153531-96-5: supplier describes neutral taste, 16% versus 12% magnesium, and different solubility behaviour between hydration forms. This makes an unnamed “magnesium citrate” purchase specification inadequate. [IN07, undated](https://www.jungbunzlauer.com/ingredients/trimagnesium-citrate/).

Compare these at equal **elemental** magnesium in the chosen base, after formulator review. Arithmetic illustration only: 50 mg elemental magnesium needs approximately 500 mg of a 10% material, 312.5 mg of a 16% material, or 416.7 mg of a 12% material. These are not recommended doses; actual batch assay and hydration state control the calculation. Compare perceived mineral taste, sourness, chalkiness/grit, body, clarity and sedimentation; a supplier's neutral-taste statement is a hypothesis for the finished recipe. Include a zero-added-mineral base and evaluate chilled and normal consumption temperature. Do not infer acute sleep induction from a normal-function magnesium claim.

**Sodium and potassium:** human work demonstrates that salt–bitter interactions vary by compound. A model-broth study found sodium chloride reduced the tested bitter off-taste while potassium chloride did not. This is not a sports-drink optimisation or permission to replace one salt with another gram-for-gram. [IN08: Breslin and Beauchamp, 1995, US](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/8788095/); [IN09: Wise et al., 2019, US](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6542971/).

Use sodium chloride and potassium chloride as explicitly identified reference materials; candidate citrate/lactate alternatives require their own specifications and testing. Record sodium/potassium **ions**, counterions and background water composition, not just “electrolytes mg”. In hydration, compare saltiness and bitter/mineral aftertaste against a water control. Whether a mineral cue is welcome after exercise but unwelcome at a desk is a customer hypothesis. A small-sip taste result cannot establish thirst satisfaction over a bottle or superiority to water.

### Botanical relaxation and bedtime

**Ashwagandha:** KSM-66's manufacturer describes a root-only extract with neutral taste. That does not verify its neutrality in an acidified processed drink, but it directly cautions against labelling all ashwagandha extracts as bitter. [IN10: manufacturer page, undated, India/global](https://ksm66ashwagandhaa.com/why-choose-ksm66.php). For a real material, record Withania somnifera identity, root versus leaf, solvent, extraction ratio, withanolide assay/method, withaferin A, carrier and microbiological specification. Earthy/rooty, bitter and drying notes are descriptors to **test**, not observed properties of a proposed can. The German BfR finds material safety uncertainties and cannot derive a safe intake from available data; attractive sensory performance would not resolve them. [IN11: BfR, 10 September 2024](https://www.bfr.bund.de/cm/349/ashwagandha-food-supplements-with-potential-health-risks.pdf). Keep ashwagandha out of the first consumer prototype flight unless the exact material and exposure have been cleared for the intended country/category.

**Lion's mane:** a 2025 UK trial used 3 g of Cambridge Commodities' 10:1 fruiting-body extract in a freshly prepared 250 mL lemon-squash/water drink; it found no overall cognition/mood benefit in 18 adults. The register records its extraction/carrier details. Commercial affiliations are disclosed. Shelf-stable sensory performance remains unknown. [IN12: Surendran et al., 10 April 2025](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/nutrition/articles/10.3389/fnut.2025.1405796/full).

For this material family, earthy/mushroom aroma, umami, brown colour, haze and sediment are **screening hypotheses**. Do not label them measured defects. Fruiting body, mycelium, extraction ratio and carrier are not interchangeable specifications. A botanical product can be flavourful without its functional extract; first decide whether the benefit evidence and customer interest justify adding material and technical cost. The trial's consumer-prepared squash is an experimental vehicle only, not an eligible commercial benchmark.

### Sweetener and body system

A 126-person US consumer study compared Reb A, D and M with sucrose in water at high sweetness. Reb A had more bitterness in that comparison; Reb M had lingering sweetness. This supports separating bitter aftertaste from sweet persistence and specifying glycoside composition, rather than a single “stevia aftertaste” verdict. It does **not** establish preference at the lower sweetness, acidity, carbonation or functional load of our drinks. [IN13: Tao and Cho, 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7466183/).

Test a modest sucrose/juice reference against an appropriately formulated reduced-sugar system at matched perceived sweetness before deciding on zero sugar. Record sugar concentration and soluble solids, but do not use °Brix as a direct measure of sweetness in mixed acid/mineral/high-intensity-sweetener systems. Choose any soluble fibre only after identifying exact chemistry/chain-length distribution, grade and target dose. Without that identity, viscosity, acid/heat stability, sensory neutrality and tolerability are **unknown**. Fibre is not required to make a premium focus drink; adding it solely for body creates another ingredient and potential tolerance variable.

## Small, affordable technical sequence

These are proposed experiments and decision rules, not published findings:

1. **Screen the base first:** outsourced beverage formulator prepares flavour bases without optional functional extracts. Choose a still and lightly sparkling candidate suited to the occasion. Fix water and ingredient lots; record formula version, pH, titratable acidity, soluble solids, carbonation, colour and serving temperature.
2. **One material at a time:** compare base versus base plus exact proposed caffeine, or equal-elemental mineral candidates. Avoid simultaneously changing salt, acid, sweetener and aroma. Use coded replicate preparations to distinguish formulation differences from mixing variation.
3. **Then test interactions:** caffeine × sweetening system for focus; mineral salt × acid system for hydration; tea level × fruit aroma for a still focus option. A four-cell bench experiment is enough to detect major practical problems before broad consumer work. A minimum technical comparison is not a national preference estimate.
4. **Use the intended process and pack:** obtain a co-packer/process-authority assessment for the actual still/sparkling matrix, heat treatment, can lining or bottle, closure and shelf life. Repeat taste and analytical checks after a pilot process and through intended storage; monitor sediment, separation, colour/aroma drift and dissolved actives. Accelerated observations can screen risk but do not alone validate shelf life or microbiological safety.
5. **Full serving comes before range selection:** use the project's consumer protocol after material clearance. A second-serving wish is a sensory/commercial measure; it is not permission to give multiple active cans. Track total exposure from all samples and other intake, and assess second servings on another day where appropriate. EFSA notes that even 100 mg caffeine near bedtime can affect some adults' sleep; its general adult reference limits are not product targets. [IN14: EFSA, 2015 assessment/current topic](https://www.efsa.europa.eu/en/topics/topic/caffeine).

For bedtime, exclude intentional caffeine, verify residual caffeine in any tea preparation, and test a still low-acid option. For social use, test both fruit-led and restrained botanical bitterness; do not assume the functional extract needs to supply the adult cue. There is no tasting evidence yet that either will win.

## CBD: research-only extension with separate gates

The main launch can be developed CBD-free. THC-led/intoxicating products remain outside scope. The separate CBD decision is not settled by a pleasing flavour or a US listing.

| Question | Current evidence and implication |
|---|---|
| Demand | Existing repository research does not establish Czech CBD-only RTD penetration, paid repeat or willingness to pay. Test desire for relaxation separately from a CBD label/concept; do not use THC drinks or CBD oils as beverage-demand proxies. [Existing market assessment](../../../market/04-eu-czech-competition/supporting/regulatory-and-evidence-update.md). |
| Effects | A 30-participant Australian insomnia pilot used 150 mg nightly **sublingual** CBD for two weeks; most sleep measures resembled placebo, with selected secondary differences. It does not support a reliable effect or dose in a low-dose aqueous can. [IN15: Narayan et al., 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11063694/). |
| Safety | February 2026 EFSA guidance is limited to qualifying ≥98% CBD **supplements without nanoparticles** and gives 0.0275 mg/kg/day (1.925 mg for 70 kg). A September 2026 positive opinion concerns a **specific synthetic CBD in MCT oil supplement**, max 2 mg/day, adults over 25, excluding pregnancy/lactation; medicine-user safety remained unestablished. Neither is an aqueous RTD assessment or efficacy claim. [IN16](https://www.efsa.europa.eu/en/news/provisional-safe-level-cannabidiol-novel-food); [IN17](https://efsa.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.2903/j.efsa.2026.10154). |
| EU/Czech permission | The Union-list review and targeted current search did not establish authorization for the intended CBD beverage. Novel-food status consultations identify CBD isolate/extract as novel; an EFSA opinion is not Commission market authorization. Czech SZPI's 29 May 2025 disclosure records sanctions/withdrawals/marketing prohibitions involving CBD foods since 2021. No-proceedings language for that particular date is not permission. [IN18](https://food.ec.europa.eu/food-safety/novel-food/authorisations/union-list-novel-foods_en), [IN19](https://food.ec.europa.eu/food-safety/novel-food/consultation-process-novel-food-status_en), [IN20](https://www.szpi.gov.cz/soubor/27-2025-redigovano.aspx). |
| Claims | No applicable authorized CBD calm/focus/sleep claim was established. Before any launch, identify the exact legal basis for every proposed claim, separately from ingredient permission. US copy and UK access are not EU precedents. [IN21: European Commission register](https://food.ec.europa.eu/food-safety/labelling-and-nutrition/nutrition-and-health-claims/eu-register-health-claims_en). |
| Sourcing/technical fit | No supplier is cleared. Isolate purity is different from final aqueous dispersibility; an oil or nanoemulsion adds carriers/emulsifiers and a different exposure/stability context. Require exact composition, particle-size distribution where relevant, food-grade traceability, cannabinoid assay with detection limits, contaminants, residual solvents, dose uniformity, packaging compatibility and end-of-life potency/cannabinoid profile. Sensory effects of an exact CBD material/emulsifier system are unknown until assessed. |

An additional reason to avoid borrowing a US recipe is a primary study first published online **2 April 2024**, in a 2025 journal issue. One specific nano-emulsified CBD isolate was added to acidic commercial foods/beverages (pH 2.6–3.7); the researchers observed Δ9-THC formation in the tested beverages during storage. This does **not** establish that every CBD emulsion converts at the same rate or in every acidified drink. It does mean a starting “THC-free” certificate cannot establish the finished drink's entire shelf-life profile. [IN22: Mullen et al., *Stability of Nano-Emulsified Cannabidiol in Acidic Foods and Beverages*](https://doi.org/10.1089/CAN.2024.0064).

**Decision:** do CBD concept/naming research separately, with no CBD ingestion in the first sensory study. Reconsider only after documented beverage authorization, exact-material safety and a qualified assessment of processing/storage stability, plus evidence of additional customer value. The cited stability research is analytical evidence, not an instruction to manufacture or serve cannabinoid drinks.
