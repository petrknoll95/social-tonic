# Czech/EU language and legal naming workstream

Research checked 17 October 2026, Europe/Prague (UTC+02:00). This is a framework review, not trademark clearance, an assessment of a finished label, or native-speaker validation. No names are declared available. Findings from the repository's claims/messaging and brand/acquisition research were treated as hypotheses and checked against the sources below. Earlier work-break recommendations do not settle this project's positioning.

## Practical conclusion

Choose a name that people can recover after hearing it and that can survive ordinary Czech conversation; then evaluate the same jobs in intended expansion markets. A shared spelling does not require identical national pronunciations. Keep the masterbrand, product family, flavour and factual drink description distinct in the architecture. A functional name can itself carry a regulated claim, so trademark registration and food-claims compliance are separate gates. None of these rules establishes which name will sell best.

## Language: facts and implications must stay separate

The proposed first language panel is Czech for launch; Slovak, German and Polish for neighbouring expansion possibilities; and French as a contrasting Romance-language check. This is an analyst-selected diagnostic panel, not a market-priority ranking or coverage of the EU. German would need market-context checks in Germany and Austria if both are planned. English is a possible naming language and international working language, not an assumed comprehension baseline. No proficiency percentage or universal preference for English is inferred.

| Language | Supported linguistic observation | Naming implication to test; not a measured consumer finding |
|---|---|---|
| Czech | ÚJČ describes foreign-name adaptation: stress commonly moves to the first syllable, unavailable sounds are replaced, and borrowed words can have variable pronunciations. A letter such as **c** cannot be assigned one universal reading in foreign names. [LEG001](https://prirucka.ujc.cas.cz/?id=914) | Record unaided readings and spelling from audio separately. English **w**, silent letters, vowel combinations and altered spellings may require learning. Accept a stable local reading if it remains recognisable and searchable; do not demand an English accent. |
| Czech in conversation | Brand-name inflection depends partly on established grammatical gender and the spoken ending. Some endings permit regular declension; names ending in spoken /i/ or /u/ generally remain indeclinable. [LEG002](https://prirucka.ujc.cas.cz/?id=398) | Test ordinary requests and references: “Dám si…”, “Máš ještě…?”, “Bez…”, “Ochutnal/a jsem…”. Ask respondents to complete them naturally. Do not treat an indeclinable brand as defective merely because it does not change. Record spontaneous nicknames, gender and case forms. |
| Slovak | The Slovake.eu teaching reference states that stress is almost always initial. [LEG003](https://slovake.eu/grammar/pronunciation/accent) | Run a separate Slovak panel. Similarity to Czech does not establish that a pun, a word ending or a social association transfers. Check Slovak meanings, diacritics, inflection and informal speech independently. |
| German | Goethe-Institut teaching material distinguishes stress patterns for stems, prefixes, foreign-word suffixes and compounds. [LEG004](https://www.goethe.de/resources/files/pdf354/gi_lauter_bitte-v2.pdf), pp. 54–55 | Test spontaneous stress and spelling rather than assuming a Czech or English reading. Check whether segmentation produces unintended ordinary words or a different compound meaning; check umlaut-free variants if present. |
| Polish | The Polish government education reference identifies penultimate stress as the common pattern and documents exceptions. [LEG005](https://zpe.gov.pl/a/przeczytaj/DQ1t5Sfjr) | A three-syllable candidate can acquire different rhythm from Czech. Test heard-to-written recovery and possible local meanings; a visually familiar consonant sequence is not evidence of the same sound. |
| French | OQLF explains tonic accent at the end of a syntactic group and distinguishes expressive accent. [LEG006](https://vitrinelinguistique.oqlf.gouv.qc.ca/24511/la-prononciation/notions-de-base-en-phonetique/laccent-tonique-et-laccent-expressif) | Test the name in a real sentence and bar order. Check sound-to-spelling recovery, final letters and meaning. This Canadian reference supports a general phonetic principle; it does not validate French-market brand associations. |

**Diacritics are a trade-off, not an automatic rejection.** They can retain local meaning and identity. Their removal can change pronunciation or cause collisions. Current CZ.NIC rules linked from its rules page restrict .cz names to a–z, digits and hyphens (§4.6). A Czech name with diacritics therefore needs a workable domain spelling. Check both spellings, keyboard entry, autocorrect and retailer databases. This does not mean every EU domain or social platform has the same rules. No domains were registered or availability claims made. [LEG007](https://www.nic.cz/files/nic/doc/20240501_Pravidla_registrace_CZ.pdf).

**Meaning requires independent testing.** Before showing the beverage, ask for meanings, associations, imagined product and spontaneous pronunciation. After showing a matched concept, ask what the drink appears to contain and do. Screen dictionary meanings, homophones, slang, offensive readings and competitor overlap with competent speakers in each market. Slang dictionaries and web searches can supply leads, but cannot substitute for documented local judgment. No candidate-specific linguistic screening occurred in this assignment.

**Social tonic and functional soda are propositions to explain.** Desk interpretation: Czech “social tonic” could evoke a quinine mixer, a general tonic, an alcohol alternative or a promised social effect; the actual distribution of interpretations is unknown. “Functional” does not identify the function. Test a plain local description beside the masterbrand, such as a truthful equivalent of “sparkling non-alcoholic drink”, then the occasion and any justified benefit. These are descriptor approaches, not cleared labels or final brand names. Translate the communication job, not only the English words. Do not presume that “calm”, “feel good” or “euphoric” retains either the same emotion or legal interpretation across languages.

## Trademark: distinctiveness is relative to the goods and the public

EUIPO distinguishes a sign that identifies trade origin from descriptive or customary wording; acquired distinctiveness is a possible evidential exception, not a cheap launch strategy. A common word can be distinctive for unrelated goods, while an invented spelling can still describe a beverage immediately. Suggestiveness and descriptiveness require contextual assessment. Current EUIPO examples illustrate how a word's relevant meaning changes with the goods. [LEG008](https://www.euipo.europa.eu/en/help-centre/tm/faq-registration), [LEG009](https://guidelines.euipo.europa.eu/2319054/2042610/trade-mark-guidelines/2-1-one-word).

EUTMR Article 7(2) permits refusal even where a ground exists only in part of the EU. Accordingly, do not screen an EU mark only in Czech and English. Article 33(7) also says that being in the same Nice class does not itself make goods similar, and different classes do not itself make them dissimilar. [LEG010](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/en/ALL/?uri=CELEX%3A32017R1001). Visual, phonetic and conceptual resemblance all matter; EUIPO's current guidance treats the purchase method as one contextual factor and rejects a blanket rule across broad categories. A spelling tweak is therefore not a reliable solution to a sound-alike conflict. [LEG011](https://guidelines.euipo.europa.eu/2319054/1983244/trade-mark-guidelines/4-impact-of-the-method-of-purchase-of-goods-and-services).

The relevant starting classification is often **32**, but the actual formula, goods and services control. WIPO's **13th edition, version 2026**, distinguishes the following. [LEG012](https://nclpub.wipo.int/esen/pdf-download.pdf?dateInForce=20260101&lang=en&tab=class_headings).

| Class | Relevance to the later search brief |
|---|---|
| 32 | Non-alcoholic drinks and beer; includes energy drinks, isotonic drinks and de-alcoholised beverages. |
| 30 | Coffee-, tea-, cocoa- and chocolate-based drinks. |
| 29 | Milk-predominating drinks and milk substitutes. |
| 5 | Dietary supplements and beverages adapted for medical purposes; a functional beverage is not automatically in this class. |
| 33 | Alcoholic drinks other than beer; potentially adjacent conflict-search context, not this core product's automatic class. |
| 35 | Relevant retail/advertising services where applicable; selling one's own goods does not by itself convert goods into a class-35 service or replace goods protection. |

**Proposed search process:** define intended territories and exact goods; search the Czech ÚPV database, EUIPO/TMview and WIPO records; search exact names, stems, phonetic alternatives, spacing, diacritic-free forms and translations where relevant; include beverage, supplement and genuinely related goods; review owner, priority, status, territory, goods and reputation; then obtain a professional similarity/registrability opinion before committing to a launch name. ÚPV recommends pre-filing searches, and its database includes Czech, EUIPO and WIPO designations for Czechia/EU; it warns that imported data can contain inaccuracies. [LEG013](https://upv.gov.cz/informacni-zdroje/narodni-databaze/databaze-ochrannych-znamek), [LEG014](https://www.upv.gov.cz/prumyslova-prava/ochranne-znamky/narodni-prihlaska-ochranne-znamky). This is a later workflow, not a search or clearance already completed.

Company registration identifies a legal business; it does not automatically register a product trademark. A domain is an address and can conflict with trademark rights. Check company name, trademark, domain and handles separately. ÚPV's WIPO-adapted guide explains these distinctions; it is older educational guidance and is not used here for current fees or procedure. Trademark rights are territorial: a Czech registration is not automatically EU-wide, and UK expansion needs a separate territory plan. [LEG015](https://upv.gov.cz/files/uploads/PDF_Dokumenty/brozury/2020/vytvoreni_oz_062015.pdf), [LEG016](https://www.wipo.int/en/web/trademarks/protection).

## Claims: a brand name does not sit outside food law

Regulation 1924/2006 Article 1(3) addresses trademarks, brand and fancy names that may be understood as nutrition/health claims: use requires an accompanying related compliant claim. Article 10(3) links general health-related wellbeing wording to a specific authorised claim. This is not permission to support any broad promise with an unrelated vitamin footnote. The Annex permits “natural/naturally” as a nutrition-claim prefix when the food naturally meets that claim's conditions; that is not a general “all natural” certification. [LEG017](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2006/1924/2014-12-13).

Regulation 1169/2011 requires accurate, non-misleading food information, restricts disease prevention/treatment/cure presentation, and requires understandable mandatory information in the destination market. [LEG018](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN-FR/TXT/?uri=CELEX%3A32011R1169). The lawful name of the food is not replaced by the masterbrand or fancy name, as the CJEU reiterates in C-595/21. [LEG024](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=celex%3A62021CJ0595).

Practical risk assessment: “calm”, “focus”, “sleep”, “energy”, “gut-friendly”, “no crash” or a botanical name beside a transformation promise may communicate a physiological benefit. The whole name/descriptor/pack/advertisement matters. A social occasion without a health implication is a different proposition; no universal banned-word list follows. Czech SZPI's guidance expressly calls for context. [LEG019](https://www.szpi.gov.cz/clanek/voditka-k-problematice-zdravotnich-a-vyzivovych-tvrzeni). The September 2026 C-721/24 judgment, in a supplement context, likewise distinguishes practical consumption information from wording that implies a health relationship. It does not clear any beverage name. [LEG023](https://juris.curia.europa.eu/juris/document/document_print.jsf?cid=8493225&docid=315283&doclang=EN&pageIndex=0&text=).

For botanical benefits, the 2025 C-386/23 judgment concerned psychological/behavioural and general wellbeing claims in supplement advertising. It does not support “on hold means permitted”; the relevant Article 28(6) transition must actually apply. Conversely, it should not be simplified into a conclusion that every botanical name or all botanical information is banned. [LEG022](https://infocuria.curia.europa.eu/tabs/redirect/juris/documents.jsf?critereEcli=ECLI%3AEU%3AC%3A2025%3A304).

Two Czech-specific issues should influence architecture:

- **Alcohol absence:** SZPI's 12 March 2025 guidance distinguishes the statutory non-alcoholic category (up to 0.5% ABV) from voluntary “ZERO”, “BEZ ALKOHOLU” and 0.0 alcohol statements, understood as no alcohol. Its 0.05% discussion concerns analytical quantification, not a separate legal allowance. The guidance excludes de-alcoholised grapevine products, which have their own regime. A future brand or family centred on zero could consequently create a permanent formula/verification dependency. Do not generalise the Czech interpretation across all EU product categories or countries. [LEG020](https://www.szpi.gov.cz/clanek/pouzivani-informaci-o-potravine-zero-alkohol-bez-alkoholu-no-alkohol-nealkoholicky-0-alkoholu-0-0-alkoholu-na-nealkoholickych-napojich.aspx).
- **Natural/clean claims:** Czech Decree 417/2016 §2 addresses potentially misleading descriptions including “přírodní” and “čistá”. It also allows demonstrably justified, non-misleading descriptive presentation. Neither a blanket ban nor an unrestricted naturalness claim is supported. A literal clean/natural masterbrand deserves context/formula review. [LEG021](https://mze.gov.cz/public/portal/mze/legislativa/vap87546-131).

The CBD-free core should be named on its own proposition. An optional CBD extension must remain a separately evaluated future variant: novel-food authorisation includes specific permitted uses, specifications and labelling, and does not follow from a trademark or retailer listing. No CBD beverage permission was assessed or established by this naming workstream. [LEG025](https://food.ec.europa.eu/food-safety/novel-food/authorisations/union-list-novel-foods_en?wt-search=yes).

## Brief and test implications

Use a flexible masterbrand only if it earns recognition and suits the chosen positioning; flexibility is not an end in itself. Benefit/ingredient specificity can improve early comprehension but creates expectation and reformulation dependencies. The proposed design comparison should keep the same hierarchy: **masterbrand → family/variant → flavour → truthful local category descriptor → separately reviewed promise**. A restrictive claim does not become unrestricted merely by moving from masterbrand to subbrand.

Before producing final candidates, settle audience, main occasion, sensory experience, realistic functional proposition and likely launch markets. Hard holds include a material unresolved trademark conflict, prohibited/misleading promise, serious offensive interpretation in a target market, or persistent confusion that the team cannot resolve through normal use. Taste preferences, fashionable sound, preferred word length and visual style are softer judgments. No numerical total should compensate for a hard hold.

For the later protocol, separate reading aloud from spelling an unseen name after audio; test delayed recall before recognition; elicit unaided associations before the product reveal; compare all candidates on identical packaging and descriptors; counterbalance order; then test natural ordering and spoken search. Analyse each language separately, recording acceptable variants rather than marking every non-English pronunciation as wrong. Use trained local reviewers for meaning/claims; use relevant consumers for comprehension. This report contains a test design, not results.

## Coverage and access limits

Official and academic/teaching sources support the language and law facts. Naming implications are analyst recommendations. No representative consumer data establishes Czech preference for English, understanding of social tonic, candidate pronunciation or emotional transfer. No complete trademark or slang screen was undertaken. Five languages omit much of the EU. Some EUR-Lex/EUIPO pages returned bot/403/empty-body responses on direct opening; specific indexed official passages were available and are explicitly marked **search_excerpt** in the ledger. The legal conclusions stay at framework level; original acts/current consolidations and candidate-specific application must be rechecked before filing or sale. WIPO's generic class URL initially exposed a not-yet-effective version, so this memo uses the explicitly dated 2026 edition instead.
