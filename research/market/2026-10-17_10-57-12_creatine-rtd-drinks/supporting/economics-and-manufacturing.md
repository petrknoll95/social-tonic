## Transparent wholesale headroom example

**Assumptions chosen only to expose the economics:** shelf price excludes deposit but includes an assumed 20% VAT; retailer gross margin 35% of its net sales; distributor gross margin 20% of its net sales; other brand variable costs €0.20/can; desired contribution before acquisition and fixed costs €0.40/can. These margins, tax rate and costs are **not observations or estimates of Czech commercial terms**. Replace them with the actual country/category/channel terms, taxes, logistics, trade spending and spoilage.

`Brand receipt = shelf price / (1 + VAT) × (1 − retailer margin) × (1 − distributor margin)`

`Maximum affordable COGS = brand receipt − other variable costs − desired contribution`

| Assumed retail price | Calculated brand receipt | COGS ceiling at those assumptions |
|---:|---:|---:|
| €1.75 | €0.758 | €0.158 |
| €2.50 | €1.083 | €0.483 |
| €3.00 | €1.300 | €0.700 |

The result is a ceiling, **not evidence that any supplier can meet it**. A €3 shelf price can leave little room for a high-cost small run. Conversely, a direct sale avoids some channel margins but incurs pick/pack, parcel shipping, payment fees, refunds and acquisition cost. “Free delivery” is still a business expense. Never compare a DTC retail receipt directly with a wholesale cost excluding these items.

For DTC use: `order contribution = collected net revenue − cans×landed COGS − fulfilment − shipping subsidy − payment fees − refunds/returns − variable marketing`. For any channel: `break-even paid units = fixed launch costs / positive contribution per paid unit`. Both are **unknown** until quotes and a credible paid-sales basis exist. Free samples are a cost, not paid sell-through.

## Inventory feasibility is part of stability

Define `saleable channel window = validated shelf life − release/transport time − retailer required remaining life`. This quantity must be positive under realistic temperature exposure. Then compare paid demand with the actual run: `required average paid units/week = units that must sell / saleable weeks`. No numerical shelf life, run size or weekly demand is assigned to this project.

Smaller, more frequent runs can reduce age at consumption but raise unit cost and scheduling demands. Higher pH may assist chemical retention in some systems but changes microbial/process requirements. Refrigeration can slow some degradation while creating cold-chain cost and precipitation risk. Overfilling adds material, initial exposure and label considerations; it is not a free correction for an unknown loss curve. These are engineering and commercial inferences requiring the tests specified in the technical review.

The evidence supports a **conditional development investigation**, not a conclusion that a shelf-stable €2–3 soda is profitable. The decisive economic question is whether retained dose, sensory quality, channel life and paid repeat can all be achieved within the same quoted cost structure.
