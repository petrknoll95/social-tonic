# Creatine RTD formulation and stability review

Research date: **17 October 2026, Europe/Prague (UTC+02:00)**. Desk research only. No formulation, assay, tasting, process validation or shelf-life study was performed. Read with [science and safety](science-and-safety.md), [technical references](technical-sources.csv) and [study extraction](../data/studies-workstream.csv). Source IDs T001–T027 identify the underlying documents.

## Decision-relevant conclusion

A finished creatine soda is technically plausible, but public evidence does **not** establish an interchangeable, shelf-stable creatine platform that a founder can adopt without development. The difficult requirement is retaining the declared amount of **intact creatine**, in the consumed serving, after processing and distribution through expiry. Dissolution at manufacture, a supplier certificate, a clear liquid, a patent and an acute absorption study answer different questions.

The strongest negative evidence is actual solution experimentation and a historical marketed-liquid assay. The strongest constructive evidence is formulation-dependent kinetics, encapsulation development and existing finished products. Neither establishes the retained content of a new can. No independent, batch-identified, end-of-shelf-life analysis of a current qualifying soda was located in this workstream. That is an evidence gap, not a finding that every current product fails.

## Separate five measurements

1. **Input:** grams of a purchased preparation put into the batch.
2. **Identity and equivalent amount:** grams of monohydrate, hydrochloride or other compound, versus grams of the creatine moiety.
3. **Physical availability in the package:** dissolved fraction, particles, sediment and serving uniformity.
4. **Chemical retention:** intact creatine versus creatinine or other products, at release and expiry.
5. **Human delivery and effect:** absorption, muscle/brain uptake, relevant outcomes and repeat-use conditions.

Do not collapse these into a single “5 g” or “bioavailable” number. AESAN's 2024 assessment distinguishes **3.41 g creatine monohydrate** and **3.84 g creatine hydrochloride**, each corresponding to approximately **3 g creatine** [T022]. A dry-blend specification and a finished drink's assay may use different bases.

## Direct liquid evidence and its boundaries

| Evidence | What was actually tested and observed | What it cannot establish |
|---|---|---|
| [Dash & Sawhney, 2002](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S073170850200167X), T001 | LC-UV separated creatine/creatinine. A dilute 80 µg/mL water system at 25°C over ten days had fitted first-order k=0.0263/day. Saturation experiments reported monohydrate 16.6±0.3 mg/mL at 25°C. | Stability pH was not reported; concentration and matrix differ substantially from soda. The solubility result is not a universal recipe limit. |
| [Ganguly et al., 2003](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1208/pt040225), T002 | Effervescent dicreatine-citrate preparation stored 45 days at 25°C/4°C; reported losses 90%/80%. Refrigerated monohydrate crystals appeared on day seven. Initial water was pH 6.2, but the **prepared liquid** began around pH 3.6, rising to 4.5 at room temperature. | Abstract-level extraction; detailed sample handling and partition of chemical loss versus precipitated material were not fully verified. Do not describe every lost gram as quantified creatinine formation. Not a sealed commercial soda. |
| [Uzzan et al., 2009](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19635041/), T003 | About 5 g/L anhydrous creatine in glycerol/pH 4-buffer models; aw 0.31–0.983. At aw 0.983, fitted half-lives were 368 d at 4°C, 43 d at 23°C and 12 d at 35°C. Curves departed from first-order behaviour later. | Model-specific half-lives, not expiry dates. Glycerol also changed measured pH/viscosity; the study included controls. Cannot use this to prescribe a universal overage or extrapolate indefinitely. |
| [Fuller & Elia, 1988](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/0009898188900964), T004 | Standard solutions and urine-storage models showed temperature/pH-dependent interconversion; initial creatine-to-creatinine rate peaked near pH 3.7. | Not a food formulation. “Lower pH is always worse” is too simple across the entire pH range. Neither extreme pH nor neutral pH establishes a viable beverage. |
| [Uzzan et al., 2007](https://experts.umn.edu/en/publications/thermal-and-storage-stability-of-nutraceuticals-in-a-milk-beverag/), T005 | Nutraceutical milk systems processed at 72–138°C with different holding times; creatine showed limited processing/storage stability. | Abstract accessed. Not permission to assign a heat-loss percentage or copy the paper's general overrun discussion into a soda formula. |
| [Saiki et al., 2025](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0963996925018101), T006 | Forced-stress, FTIR/HRMS work found matrix-dependent degradation profiles beyond the conventional creatinine pathway. | Abstract accessed; no defensible quantified beverage retention or toxicological threshold extracted. Its acidic-condition finding does not overturn other quantified studies using different conditions and methods. |

The often-repeated three-day pH-loss numbers (approximately 4%, 12%, 21% at pH 5.5, 4.5, 3.5) should be attributed to their chain: the [Howard/Harris 1999 patent](https://patents.google.com/patent/US5968544A/en), T007, describes beverage-like solutions, HPLC and temperature-controlled experiments; later reviews repeat/adapt the findings. They are **one historical experimental source**, not several independent replications and not modern brand assays. The patent also investigated refrigeration and precipitation. It does not validate a commercial shelf life today.

An old finished-liquid warning exists: T001 tested **Creatine Serum, Muscular Marketing**, bought at a General Nutrition Center. Its liquid drops contained **1.71±0.18% of label-claimed creatine**, with creatinine predominant; pH 4.1, three analytical replicates. The study was supported by FSI Nutrition. This is historical analytical failure of an excluded concentrate/drop format, not a qualifying soda or proof of a current brand's failure.

## Dose-to-volume and physical stability

Illustrative concentration arithmetic, **not a formula recommendation**:

| Declared compound input | 250 mL | 330 mL | 355 mL |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| 3 g creatine monohydrate |12.00 g/L|9.09 g/L|8.45 g/L|
| 5 g creatine monohydrate |20.00 g/L|15.15 g/L|14.08 g/L|

Formula: grams ÷ litres. These figures are compound mass, not automatically anhydrous creatine equivalents. They show why can size and temperature matter. A room-temperature solubility value cannot certify a chilled can; acidification, salts, particles, protective carriers and other solids change the system. Warming may speed dissolution while also increasing chemical reaction; subsequent cooling may permit crystallisation. Actual settling or grittiness must be measured, not inferred from the ingredient list.

An apparently clear liquid is not proof of retained dose: creatinine may remain in solution. Conversely, a dispersion is not automatically defective if it is lawful, safe, palatable and delivers a uniform declared dose. For sparkling products, instructions to shake cannot be casually borrowed from still products. Assess realistic unopened transport and pouring, including whether meaningful active remains in sediment or on package surfaces.

## Creia and CreaBev: what the public evidence actually says

Creia's [current variety-pack page](https://creiadrinks.com/products/variety-pack), T009, markets **5 g CreaBev** and lists monohydrate, soluble pea fibre and sunflower lecithin. It calls its CreaBev stability curve an **estimate** from supplier testing at its target pH, says independent testing of its cans is underway, and describes higher pH, shorter production cycles and filling above the declared quantity. Actual numeric pH, overage, process, storage specification and finished-can assay were not supplied there. Those statements acknowledge a stability problem; they do not resolve it.

The older [CreaBev technical marketing page](https://creatine.com/science/), T008, distinguishes a 68% monohydrate neutral version and 74% acidic version with different carriers. It reports 70% monohydrate remaining after 12 months in a neutral system and 30% at pH 2.8, and a 108-person sensory panel preferring CreaBev over HCl but showing similar results to ordinary monohydrate. Full protocols/raw data were not available; references to planned 2021 research and old pricing make current specification confirmation essential. Its price is **not a current quote**.

**Conditional calculation only:** if 5 g meant preparation mass and the actual material were 74% monohydrate, it would contain 3.70 g monohydrate before processing, or approximately 3.25 g creatine using the AESAN 3/3.41 conversion. At 68%, the corresponding values would be 3.40 g and approximately 2.99 g. Neither calculation establishes Creia's actual formula, because dose basis, material version, deliberate overage and retention are unknown. Obtain the production specification and finished-can assay before calculating cost per retained gram or claim eligibility.

The [2022 CreaBev pharmacokinetic trial](https://assets.cureus.com/uploads/original_article/pdf/83527/20220523-15441-1ndk31g.pdf), T010, matters because its abstract is easy to overread. Thirty-two men were analysed after freshly mixed powders providing nominally equal 5 g monohydrate. Full-text Table 3 found significant AUC/Cmax differences **between CreaBev 2 and CreaBev 1**, but **neither differed significantly from ordinary monohydrate**. Supplier employees were coauthors. No muscle uptake, chronic performance or stored-can stability was measured. This is not human proof that 26% less creatine delivers equivalent benefit.

The [2021 CreaBev study](https://ecronicon.net/assets/ecnu/pdf/ECNU-16-00992.pdf), T011, used powder mixed daily, not aged cans. Its small 28-day comparison found no GI-score advantage over ordinary monohydrate or no supplement. A within-group cognitive change cannot by itself establish superiority. The no-product control also limits blinding despite the paper's description. These limitations matter more than repeating its marketing-ready conclusion.

The [Glanbia 2019 patent publication](https://patents.google.com/patent/WO2019079651A1/en), T012, describes polysaccharide/lecithin colloidal systems. This is primary evidence of claimed technical development, not independent proof of CreaBev's current manufacturing specification, freedom to operate, safety authorisation or any customer's commercial retention. Patent-family legal status requires a separate qualified review.

## Alternative forms and claims

[Gufford et al., 2010](https://doi.org/10.3109/19390211.2010.491507), T013, measured greater aqueous solubility for HCl and other salts; Caco-2 permeability was not significantly greater for HCl than monohydrate. Different saturation pH values confound simplistic solubility comparisons. Solubility is not human muscle uptake or months of retention. Dicreatine citrate's improved initial dissolution did not prevent the precipitation observed in T002. Micronisation should likewise not be assumed to prevent dissolved-molecule degradation.

No verified long-term finished-soda evidence was located here for creatine citrate, HCl, nitrate, buffered creatine, ethyl ester or a preparation sold as CreaSolv. This is a coverage finding, not proof none exists. Exact identity, equivalent dose, impurity specification, human evidence and EU food-use status must be checked separately; brand names and salts are not interchangeable.

## Carbonation, packaging and process: unresolved rather than invented

No controlled study located here isolated the effect of carbonation while matching pH, temperature, dissolved creatine, formulation and package. It is therefore unjustified to assign CO₂ an independent preservation benefit or degradation penalty from this evidence. Measure pH in a defined condition, dissolved CO₂, titratable acidity and headspace consistently; degassing can change what is being measured.

Can light protection or a sound bottle closure cannot stop an intrinsic aqueous reaction. Nevertheless, the actual pack matters for pressure, sealing, migration, corrosion, light/oxygen exposure, sediment recovery and sensory drift. The studies reviewed do not select aluminium, glass or PET as universally best. Packaging compatibility and food-contact documentation must match the final matrix and process.

A microbial preservation process and a chemically gentle process must both work. Raising pH or removing a thermal step is not a validated solution. Neither a supplier's “heat stable” description nor a short hold experiment proves retention across batching, warm holds, filling, cooling and warehouse storage. Supplier/manufacturer work and quotes are required for each realistic architecture: ambient acidified sparkling, validated higher-pH aseptic system, or refrigerated short-life product. Their cost/expiry tradeoffs remain unknown.

## Proposed evidence package before a sellable pilot

The following is a proposed protocol outline, **not work completed**, and needs a qualified food formulator, process authority, analytical laboratory and legal review.

1. **Define the quantity:** exact material code/version, complete carriers and processing history, creatine-equivalent basis, purity/contaminant specifications and permitted food use. State whether declared grams are input, compound or available creatine. Require traceable production lots.
2. **Develop the method:** a matrix-suitable, stability-indicating LC method quantifying intact creatine and creatinine separately, with reference standards, specificity, recovery, precision, calibration, limits of detection/quantification, sample stability and measurement uncertainty. T001 supplies a precedent, not a plug-in validation for every soda. Confirm encapsulated-particle extraction and recovery from the whole can; distinguish dissolved and total fractions. Include mass-balance investigation and additional analytes if stress results warrant them.
3. **Measure manufacturing losses:** assay representative raw blend, pre-process liquid, post-process/fill and release product. Log full time/temperature history, pH, aw if relevant, CO₂, fill volume and lot-to-lot variability. Do not assume input mass survives processing.
4. **Run packaged real-time studies:** multiple representative production lots, intended market storage plus justified hot/cold and transport excursions, each flavour and package. Sample often enough to resolve the initial rate, physical changes and the proposed expiry; retain controls. Accelerated models require validation against real-time data and known reaction/physical behaviour.
5. **Evaluate the whole product:** microbiology and process validation, seal/package integrity and migration, chemical dose, degradation products, sediment/precipitation, turbidity, particle size where relevant, colour/aroma, sweetness/acidity and complete-serving sensory acceptance. Assess realistic pouring and residual sediment.
6. **Set release/expiry rules:** acceptance criteria derive from the truthful declaration, analytical uncertainty, authorised claim conditions, safety assessment and retailer minimum remaining life. Do not use 50% retention as a commercial expiry just because a paper reports a half-life. Overage is not established here; it can increase initial exposure, costs and degradants while leaving an expiry failure unresolved.
7. **Separate efficacy:** if the preparation or aged matrix needs bridging beyond established monohydrate evidence, commission appropriate human work only after safety/legal review. Plasma peaks alone do not substantiate a stronger performance or cognitive promise.

Quotes are required for formulation development, raw material, process trials, analytical validation, real-time and accelerated stability, microbiological validation, package tests, production minima and remaining shelf-life guarantees. A technology with better retention may still lose commercially to fresh-mixed powder if testing, cold chain, short runs or expiry write-offs dominate margin.
