# Empirical naming evidence and non-beverage mechanisms

Research completed 17 October 2026 for integration into the main report. Read repository README, research index and shared coding framework before coding. No final name proposed. Companion [empirical.json](empirical.json) contains 12 brands, commercial evidence and source metadata. Source IDs map into the integrated sources register.

## What the evidence permits

A name can affect initial interpretation, ease of processing and some memory tasks. These are intermediate outcomes, not evidence that a name determines commercial success. Separate (a) unaided recall, (b) recognition when the name is shown, (c) liking, (d) ability to reproduce spelling after hearing it, and (e) purchase behavior. An advantage on one can conflict with another.

| Study and evidence class | Finding relevant to this project | Context and limits |
|---|---|---|
| [Meyers-Levy 1989, EMP001](https://academic.oup.com/jcr/article-abstract/16/2/197/1800447), two experiments | Frequent words with numerous associations can create competing retrieval routes; less common words can produce distinctive encoding. Familiarity is not automatically better memory. | Abstract only. Outcome was memory for brand information. Do not prescribe rare dictionary words universally; pronunciation and meaning may change the trade-off. |
| [Lowrey, Shrum & Dubitsky 2003, EMP002](https://uploads.strikinglycdn.com/files/55a920cc-934e-4933-b75f-2fa2386a1fb6/Lowrey%20et%20al%202003%20JA%20Phonetic.pdf?id=101109), observational copytesting | In 480 brands, semantic fit, wordplay and initial plosives related positively to memory for less familiar brands; unusual spelling was positive, blending negative. | US women 18–65, established national brands, television exposure. Combined recall/recognition measure; observational controls cannot identify a causal naming effect. Auditory spelling/search was not tested. |
| [Yorkston & Menon 2004, EMP003](https://web.stanford.edu/class/linguist62n/yorkston.pdf), experiments | Changing one vowel in fictional ice-cream names affected perceived richness/creaminess and evaluations; information about whether a name was diagnostic could moderate the effect. | Student laboratory research, one category and two names. Study 1 had 126 undergraduates. Attribute judgments and purchase intentions are not tasted-product performance, retail conversion or long-term sales. |
| [Lowrey & Shrum 2007, EMP004](https://uploads.strikinglycdn.com/files/55a920cc-934e-4933-b75f-2fa2386a1fb6/Lowrey%20Shrum%202007%20JCR%20Phonetic.pdf?id=101104), two experiments | The preferred vowel depended on which attributes were desirable for the category. Sound–meaning fit can matter more than a universally “good” sound. | Fictitious paired names and stated preference. Useful as a hypothesis generator; insufficient to claim that a particular vowel creates calm, sociability or actual functional efficacy. |
| [Shrum et al. 2012, EMP005](https://uploads.strikinglycdn.com/files/55a920cc-934e-4933-b75f-2fa2386a1fb6/Shrum%20et%20al%202012%20IJRM%20Phonetic.pdf?id=101100), cross-language experiments | Category-dependent vowel preferences appeared across French-, Spanish- and Chinese-speaking English bilinguals. | Respective samples 106, 88 and 181; artificial names for vehicles/tools. Selected bilingual samples do not validate Czech consumers, all EU languages, emotional translations or actual drink purchases. Some individual conditions were weaker than the overall pattern. |
| [Laham, Koval & Alter 2012, EMP006](https://pages.stern.nyu.edu/~aalter/namefluency.pdf), experiments plus observation | Pronunciation ease was associated with more positive impressions across five studies. | Personal names and people, including an observational law-firm hierarchy analysis. This is indirect support for testing fluency, not direct proof that easy beverage names increase sales or that foreign names cannot work. |
| [Keller, Heckler & Houston 1998, EMP007](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/002224299806200105), laboratory advertising experiment | A benefit-suggestive television name improved recall of the matching benefit but reduced recall of a later unrelated benefit. | Abstract accessed. Supports a possible attentional cost of narrow benefit meaning; not proof that all descriptive names block extensions. It concerns message recall, not the commercial success of a product range. |
| [Rightsizing brand names 2026, EMP008](https://researchportal.vub.be/en/publications/rightsizing-brand-names-the-effects-of-brand-name-length-on-brand/), experiment | Longer invented names were better recognized; attitude followed a different, nonlinear relationship with phonemic length. | 98 participants evaluated 36 fictional car, fridge and smartphone brands. Repeated observations are not independent participants. Abstract only; forced-choice recognition differs from free recall. The [author policy brief, EMP009](https://smit.research.vub.be/en/policy-brief-94-rightsizing-brand-names-the-effects-of-brand-name-length-on-brand-name-attitude-and) is the same study, not replication. |
| [Aaker & Keller 1990, EMP010](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/pdf/10.1177/002224299005400102), brand-extension experiments | Perceived category fit and parent-brand quality were associated with extension evaluations. | Publisher abstract obtained through search; full article not accessed. Brand-level associations and perceived capabilities matter alongside the word itself. The evidence does not establish that an abstract lexical name alone grants permission to enter every category. |

These are selected studies, not a systematic review or meta-analysis. Several foundational studies are old; their mechanisms warrant testing in current Czech-language, audio-search and retail contexts. No pooled effect size is justified. Sound symbolism has multiple studies, but the same author networks and related experimental paradigms are not independent evidence for a universal naming formula.

## Naming approaches are a toolkit, not a ranking

The following trade-offs are analytical/practitioner guidance informed by the evidence above and the observed cases. They are not a league table of strategies that predict sales.

| Approach | Possible contribution | Main cost or validation question |
|---|---|---|
| Descriptive / benefit-led | Supplies a category or promise early; may reinforce congruent copy | Generic/descriptive legal weakness; too narrow a benefit can bias later message recall; benefit can become a regulated claim |
| Suggestive / associative | Offers a relevant direction without a complete product definition | Association may be weak, culturally variable or similar to many competitors |
| Arbitrary real word | Existing word and spelling combined with category difference | Ordinary search meaning competes; initial category often opaque; learned familiarity can hide launch cost |
| Invented / altered spelling | Can create a more distinctive verbal form | Recognition advantage does not guarantee spelling from audio; sound and orthography may travel differently |
| Compound | Combines distinct ideas, e.g. category plus membership | Components can create length, linguistic awkwardness or clutter; not all compounds are equally memorable |
| Founder / personal-name form | Can feel human and accommodate multiple categories | Unknown personal names carry little initial category meaning; do not invent a founder provenance |
| Place-based | Can evoke terrain, cultural origin or a world of use | Pronunciation, geography and authenticity expectations; no guarantee consumers share the image |
| Emotional / experiential / occasion | Frames a desired moment and leaves formula flexible | May overpromise mood change or disappear among generic wellness words |
| Provocative / humorous | Can create attention and a distinctive voice | Attention is not favorable interpretation, conversion or repeat purchase; cross-language humor is fragile |
| Ingredient-led | Clarifies a genuinely defining ingredient | Formula changes and range extensions can create conflict; legal claims may arise from the full presentation |
| Metaphor / cultural reference | Compact story, identity or analogy | Reference may be unknown, misread or culturally narrow; story effectiveness must be measured rather than assumed |

## Non-beverage cases that sharpen interpretation

The 12 predetermined non-beverage names are mechanism comparators, not a representative set or causal success study. Their main analytical roles are:

- **Apple and Amazon:** category-opaque masterbrands can support many product/service families. Apple reports approximately $416bn FY25 revenue; Amazon reports $716.9bn 2025 company sales, including AWS. Such scale is an outcome of entire businesses. It cannot be assigned to a lexical choice. Amazon itself documents the river/breadth analogy; this workstream does not repeat unverified Apple naming folklore. [CON001](https://www.apple.com/newsroom/2025/10/apple-reports-fourth-quarter-results/), [CON002](https://ir.aboutamazon.com/news-release/news-release-details/2026/Amazon-com-Announces-Fourth-Quarter-Results/default.aspx), [CON003](https://www.aboutamazon.com/news/books-and-authors/books-are-in-our-dna).
- **IKEA, Nike and Patagonia:** invented/acronym, mythological and geographic forms coexist with established commercial presence. IKEA reports EUR44.6bn FY25 system retail sales. NIKE Brand FY25 revenue was $44.714bn, down 9%; established does not mean perpetually growing. Patagonia's 50-year milestone establishes longevity, not its sales. [CON004](https://www.ikea.com/global/en/our-business/how-we-work/year-in-review-fy25/), [CON006](https://investors.nike.com/sec-filings/all-sec-filings/content/0001308179-25-000564/0001308179-25-000564.pdf), [CON007](https://www.patagoniaworks.com/press/2023/3/7/patagonia-celebrates-50-years-of-business-unusual).
- **Dove and Warby Parker:** an associative bird word and a four-syllable literary personal-name construction can carry coherent brands. Warby Parker's 323 FY25 year-end stores demonstrate that a DTC origin does not fix its distribution model. The founders explicitly identify the two Kerouac characters combined into its name; it is not two founders' surnames. [CON009](https://www.unilever.com/news/news-search/2023/behind-the-brand-doves-products-purpose-and-commitment-to-care/), [CON011](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1504776/000150477626000003/warbyparkerincearningsrele.htm), [CON012](https://www.warbyparker.com/culture).
- **Dollar Shave Club and Casper:** different naming styles both coexist with commercial scale and setbacks. DSC's 2015 revenue was reported as $152m, attributed to company figures; historical Unilever impairment/sale context prevents treating its acquisition story as uncomplicated success. Casper's Q3 2021 revenue of $156.5m coexisted with $25.3m net loss. Later narrowing toward mattresses illustrates that broad words do not guarantee a viable broad range. Current Casper availability means it must not be described as discontinued. [CON015](https://www.cooley.com/-/media/cooley/pdf/reprints/unilevertoacquiredollarshaveclubfor1b-reports.pdf), [CON017](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1598674/000110465921138658/tm2132922d1_ex99-1.htm), [CON018](https://www.retaildive.com/news/casper-sleep-sold-acquired-mattress-manufacturer-carpenter-co/731847/), [CON019](https://casper.com/products/casper-one-foam). The DSC impairment entry is search-excerpt-only and should remain supplementary, not headline evidence.
- **SPANX, Glossier and Away:** altered spelling, a gloss association and an occasion word illustrate different learning costs. SPANX has longevity and a current broader apparel range; Glossier's retailer list and Away's active travel assortment establish presence, not audited current scale. These cases do not establish that consumers can spell the names after a podcast mention. [CON016](https://spanx.com/pages/about-us), [CON020](https://www.glossier.com/pages/authorized-retailers), [CON022](https://www.awaytravel.com/pages/press).

## Specific cautions for integration

1. **Classification is conditional.** Established means documented scale or longevity in a specified period. Casper is coded active_scale_unknown today with an explicit historical struggling period. Dollar Shave Club is established on historical revenue, with current scale unverified. Glossier and Away are active_scale_unknown in this dataset, not declared failures.
2. **Language columns describe form.** Unknown founder intent is left unknown. IKEA, Amazon, Dove and Warby Parker have company-documented origin accounts. Patagonia's geography and Nike's mythology are coded associations without asserting an unverified selection anecdote. Casper is a personal-name form; the code is not evidence it names a founder.
3. **Syllables require a reading.** Counts use ordinary US/English brand readings, with exceptions/ambiguities noted. Patagonia is five on that reading; a Czech rendering can differ. Do not compare these numbers as a language-independent scale.
4. **Descriptor independence excludes acquired fame.** Apple may be instantly recognized by today's respondents, but the word alone does not literally describe electronics. The coding asks how a reader unfamiliar with the brand would infer category.
5. **No direct DTC discovery causal evidence found.** Search distinctiveness, handle availability, packaging hierarchy and bar-order fluency are practical design constraints. This workstream found no controlled evidence that a free domain, short handle, striking wordmark or category-keyword name alone improves beverage conversion or repeat purchase.
6. **Use matched operational tests.** Visual spelling recall, audio-to-spelling, delayed free recall, recognition and association elicitation must be separate measures. Test names before product exposure, then with equally treated packaging/descriptors. A name can improve recognition yet cause wrong-category guesses or audio spelling errors.
7. **Do not force an empirical rule from a mechanism.** These studies reject blanket claims such as shorter always wins, invented always protects expansion, easy pronunciation guarantees success, unusual spelling is always bad, or a broad name eliminates brand-extension risk.
